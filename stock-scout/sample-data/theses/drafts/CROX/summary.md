# CROX — Crocs, Inc.

## Executive summary

**What the company does.** Crocs makes the foam clog. Factories in Vietnam and a few
other countries mould it for very little money, and Crocs sells it — about half through
its own shops and website, half through other retailers — for a great deal more. In 2022
it also bought a second shoe brand, HEYDUDE, which has not worked out.

**Why it might keep making money for a long time.** For every €10 of shoes it sells,
roughly €6 is left after paying to make them. That is a remarkable gap for a simple
plastic shoe, and it exists for one reason: people want *this* shoe, with its name on the
strap, and will pay for it. The clog has now been through a full fashion cycle — it was
huge in 2006, unfashionable for a decade, and came back from 2017 — which is genuine
evidence that it is a category rather than a passing craze. Last year the company turned
about $659 million of real cash out of the business, and it is spending that cash on two
sensible things: buying back its own shares (the number of shares fell about 11% in a
single year, so each remaining share now owns a bigger slice) and paying down debt.

**What you'd actually be paying.** At today's price the whole company, including its
debt, costs roughly eleven times the cash it produces in a year. In plain terms: the
market is not asking the business to grow. It is only asking it not to shrink. That is
the interesting thing here — and it is a description of the price, not a prediction about
it. Nobody in this report is telling you what the shares are worth.

**What would make us leave.** Three things, decided in advance so they cannot be argued
away later:

1. **The cash dries up.** If the cash left over from each euro of sales falls by more than
   a third and stays there, the engine is broken.
2. **The main brand starts shrinking.** HEYDUDE shrinking is a known problem. The core
   Crocs brand shrinking for two quarters in a row would mean the thing we actually
   bought is going the same way.
3. **The debt goes back up.** The company borrowed heavily in 2022 to buy HEYDUDE and
   later had to admit that $738 million of that price was worth nothing. If it starts
   levering up again, the lesson didn't take.

Three softer signals bring it back to the desk for a look rather than triggering a sale:
the profit margin narrowing (tariffs are already costing real money), the share buyback
stopping, and credible evidence that the clog is genuinely going out of fashion rather
than just having a quiet season.

**What it costs to be wrong.** A lot, and quickly. In early 2020 this share price fell
three-quarters in ten weeks. It fully recovered, and that recovery is the difference
between a scare and a disaster — but the next fall of that size would feel exactly the
same on the way down. This is a fashion business selling something nobody needs. Its
sales and profits swing violently from year to year: in a typical year, growth misses its
own long-run average by more than that average itself. That means no honest person can
put a precise value on it, and anyone who shows you a confident ten-year forecast for
Crocs is showing you a spreadsheet, not a fact.

**The one thing worth sitting with.** The same management team that is now returning
cash so impressively is the team that spent $2.5 billion on HEYDUDE and wrote off $738
million of it. The cash today is real. The judgement that will decide what happens to the
next $2 billion of it is the open question, and no metric in this file answers it.
